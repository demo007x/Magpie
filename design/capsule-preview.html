The Architecture of Text Interfaces

When a reader encounters an unfamiliar term, the natural reflex is to pause and reach for a dictionary, breaking the flow of comprehension. The cost of that interruption compounds across a page: each lookup pulls attention away from the argument being built and toward a separate tool with its own interface, its own history, its own gravity.

A reading surface that understands text need not demand that the reader leave it. The gesture already exists — selection is how we mark what interests us, and it is performed ten thousand times a day without any expectation of consequence. Treating that gesture as a request for help is a bet worth making, but only if the help arrives without occupying the very space the reader was trying to look at.

This is the entire difficulty in one sentence. Any affordance attached to selection is, by construction, an obstruction to reading. The answer is not to guess better which selections deserve an answer; it is to make the thing that appears smaller, quieter, and far easier to get rid of.
